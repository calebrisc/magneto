Anatomy of a Mouse

The nine systems

01 Shell & chassis Holds every other part in a fixed relationship, and gives your hand something to hold.

What's inside it

  • Top shellThe grip surface. On most mice the main button plates are part of this piece, joined by a thinned strip of plastic that acts as a hinge.
  • Bottom shellThe reference plane for the whole machine. Everything's height is measured from here.
  • Screw bossesMolded columns the case screws thread into. Usually hidden under the feet.
  • PCB standoffsShort pillars that set the board's exact height — which sets the sensor's height.
  • Internal ribsThin walls stiffening large unsupported spans.
  • Surface finishTexture cut into the mold, or a sprayed rubberized coating.

How it works

The bottom shell is the datum. Every critical vertical dimension in a mouse — sensor height above the desk, switch travel, how far the wheel protrudes — is referenced to that one surface. This is why a mouse with a flexible bottom shell tracks inconsistently when you press down hard: you're bending the datum.

The ribs matter more than they look. A large flat span with no rib behind it will flex under a grip, and that flex shows up as creaking, as mushy clicks, and as the shell rubbing on the wheel.

In your build

3D printed, which frees you from every injection-molding constraint that shapes commercial mice: no draft angles, no uniform wall thickness, undercuts allowed, ribs anywhere you want them.

Splitting this into a rigid internal chassis plus a swappable outer skin means shape iteration stays cheap after the electronics are settled.

02 Buttons & switches Turn a finger press into a digital event — and it's messier than it sounds.

What's inside it

  • Button plateThe lever your finger presses.
  • HingeUsually a "living hinge" — a deliberately thinned section of the shell that bends a few million times before it fatigues.
  • Stem / plungerTransfers plate motion down to the switch.
  • MicroswitchThe sensing element. Omron D2FC, Kailh GM, Huano and TTC are the common families.
  • Leaf springInside the switch. Provides both the return force and the tactile snap.
  • ContactsOne movable, two fixed. Usually silver or gold plated.

How a microswitch actually works

A curved metal leaf is held under compression. The plunger pushes it until it passes a buckling point, at which the leaf snaps over-center and slams its movable contact against the fixed one. That snap is the click sound and the tactile break — both are the spring buckling, not the electrical event.

When the contacts meet, the metal physically bounces. They chatter open and closed for somewhere between one and ten milliseconds before settling. Firmware has to debounce this — wait out the chatter before believing the press — and that waiting period is pure added latency, sitting in the critical path of every click you make.

Why mice double-click as they age

Contacts oxidize and pit with use, so the bounce gets longer and dirtier. Eventually the chatter outlasts the debounce window and the firmware reports a second click that your finger never made. The switch isn't broken in any dramatic way — its bounce simply outgrew the filter.

Optical switches

Replace the contacts with an infrared emitter and photodiode, and put a shutter on the plunger. Breaking the beam is the event. No bounce, no debounce, no oxidation, no aging double-click. The feel, though, still comes from a conventional leaf spring — optical switches change the electrical half only.

In your build

Neither. A coil or Hall sensor reads the plate's travel as a continuous value, and an LRA generates the click sensation as a waveform. Nothing bounces, so there is no debounce, no contact wear, and no fixed actuation point — the threshold becomes a setting.

03 Scroll assembly A rotation sensor, a tactile ratchet, and a third button, stacked into one module.

What's inside it

  • WheelA plastic hub with a rubber or TPU tire over it.
  • ShaftRuns through the encoder body, carrying the rotation.
  • EncoderConverts rotation into electrical pulses.
  • Detent ringA toothed ring the wheel turns against.
  • Detent springA sprung arm riding those teeth. This, alone, is the "tick" you feel.
  • Middle-click switchSits under the encoder. The whole encoder body floats and presses down onto it.

How rotation becomes scroll

All three encoder types produce the same thing: two square-wave signals, A and B, ninety degrees out of phase. Which one changes first tells you the direction. Counting the edges tells you how far. That's quadrature encoding, and it's the same principle in a $5 mouse and a CNC machine.

  • MechanicalTwo wiper contacts sweeping a segmented metal ring. Cheap, and it wears and bounces exactly like a switch does — a scroll wheel that jumps backward is a worn encoder.
  • OpticalA slotted disc spinning between an IR emitter and two detectors. No contact, no wear, no bounce.
  • MagneticA diametrically magnetized magnet on the shaft over a Hall array. Contactless and very high resolution.

The detent feel is completely independent of the sensing — it's just a spring arm on a toothed ring. That separation is why free-spin wheels are possible: a clutch lifts the arm off the teeth, and the encoder never notices.

Electromagnetic detents — the modern version

The newest approach throws out the spring arm, the toothed ring, and the clutch entirely, and generates the detents with magnets instead. Logitech ships this as MagSpeed. Two electromagnets sit above a toothed steel rim, and which way you drive their coils decides whether the wheel is notchy or free.

Why teeth produce notches: magnetic flux strongly prefers steel over air. With a tooth directly under a pole the gap is full of steel — an easy path, a low-energy position. With a valley there it's mostly air — a harder path, higher energy. So the wheel has a natural resting position at every tooth, and rotating away from one costs energy you feel as resistance.

This is the same effect as cogging torque in a brushless motor — the notchiness when you spin an unpowered drone motor by hand. Motor designers work hard to eliminate it. Here it's the product.

A detent is not friction

Worth being precise about, because it's the difference between a wheel that feels crisp and one that feels stiff.

In your build

An LRA can only produce a vibration impulse — it cannot produce torque, so it can't hold the wheel in a notch. On its own it would tick, but the wheel would feel loose and keep drifting.

So: electromagnetic coils for the force, LRA for the texture. The coils give real counter-torque and the mode toggle with no moving parts; the LRA layers a crisp transient on top of each detent. Nobody ships that combination. And since the coils can also be energized to brake, spin-down time becomes a software setting rather than a property of your bearings.

04 Sensor & optics A very small, very fast camera that measures how far the desk moved underneath it.

What's inside it

  • Sensor ICAn image sensor and a dedicated image processor in one package. PixArt makes nearly all of them.
  • Lens assemblyOne molded part doing two jobs: aiming the illuminator at the desk, and imaging the desk onto the pixel array.
  • IlluminatorAn infrared LED in most mice, a VCSEL laser in some.
  • ApertureThe window in the bottom shell.
  • Z-height featureThe lens's mounting boss, which fixes the distance from lens to desk.

How it works

The illuminator lights the desk at a low grazing angle, so the surface's microscopic texture throws long shadows. The lens images that shadow pattern onto a tiny pixel array — on the order of thirty by thirty pixels. The onboard processor captures frames at many thousands per second and cross-correlates each new frame against the previous one, searching for the offset where the two patterns line up best.

That offset, in pixels, scaled by a factor, is your dX and dY. DPI is that scaling factor — nothing more mysterious than a multiplier applied to a measured pixel shift.

The three classic failures, explained

Glass and gloss don't track because there's no surface texture to cast shadows — the camera sees a featureless field and has nothing to correlate. The IPS limit is the speed at which consecutive frames stop overlapping enough to find a match. Lift-off distance is the height at which the image defocuses past the point the processor trusts it, so it stops reporting motion.

Sensors also drop their frame rate when idle to save power — "rest modes." Set too aggressively, the first movement after a pause feels sluggish while the sensor spins back up.

In your build

Buy the sensor as a pre-built module with its matched lens. That lens-to-desk distance is a couple of millimetres with a tolerance measured in tenths, and getting it wrong is the single most common reason homemade mice track badly.

05 Mainboard & MCU The brain, the wiring, and the loop that runs a few thousand times a second.

What's inside it

  • MCUProcessor, radio, USB controller and peripherals in one chip.
  • OscillatorThe timebase. Polling rate accuracy is only as good as this crystal.
  • FlashStores firmware plus your config: DPI steps, profiles, curves.
  • SWD padsProgramming and debug points, often just bare pads you clip onto.
  • PassivesDecoupling caps, resistors, pull-ups. Unglamorous, and the majority of the part count.
  • FFC connectorsFlat flex cables out to daughterboards — side buttons, sometimes the sensor.

What the firmware actually does

The main loop is short: read the sensor, read the buttons, assemble an HID report, hand it to USB or the radio. Repeat. An HID mouse report is remarkably small — a bitfield for the buttons, a delta for X, a delta for Y, a delta for the wheel.

Polling rate is simply how often that report goes out. 1000 Hz means one report every millisecond. Everything else — sensor reads, debounce, report assembly — has to fit inside that window with room to spare, which is the entire reason processor headroom matters at high polling rates.

06 Radio & antenna Getting a tiny packet to the PC, on time, every time.

What's inside it

  • TransceiverA 2.4 GHz radio, these days built into the MCU rather than a separate chip.
  • AntennaUsually a trace etched right into the PCB. Sometimes a tiny ceramic chip part.
  • Matching networkA few very small inductors and capacitors tuning the antenna to 50 ohms.
  • Keep-out zoneA region with no copper pour, no battery, no metal. Empty space is a component here.
  • ReceiverThe dongle: a second MCU with a radio on one side and USB on the other.

Why gaming mice ship a dongle

Bluetooth LE is standardized and frugal, but its connection-interval rules make consistently high polling rates awkward. So gaming mice run proprietary 2.4 GHz protocols instead — Nordic's ESB, Logitech's Lightspeed — paired to their own receiver. That gives full control over packet timing, retries, and channel hopping, at the cost of needing the dongle in the box.

Your hand is an antenna problem

A hand is mostly saltwater, which absorbs and detunes 2.4 GHz badly. So does a lithium pouch and any metal weight. The antenna almost always ends up at the front or a corner of the board — as far from your palm as the shape allows.

07 Power Turning a battery whose voltage sags all day into rails that don't.

What's inside it

  • CellA lithium-polymer pouch, typically 300–500 mAh in a lightweight mouse.
  • Protection circuitA sliver of PCB on the cell's own tabs guarding against over-charge, over-discharge and short circuit. Not optional.
  • Charge ICRuns the constant-current then constant-voltage charge profile from USB.
  • Load-shareLets the mouse run off USB while charging, instead of cycling the cell every time you plug in.
  • RegulatorA LiPo swings from 4.2 V down to 3.0 V; the electronics want a steady rail. Buck-boost or LDO.
  • Fuel gaugeModels the actual state of charge, so the reported percentage isn't just a voltage guess.

Where the current actually goes

The radio and the sensor dominate. Both spend most of their life asleep and wake in bursts — which is why battery life quoted at 1000 Hz and at 125 Hz differ so wildly, and why a mouse idling on your desk can last weeks.

Voltage sag under load is the sneaky one: a peak current draw pulls the rail down momentarily, and if the regulator and bulk capacitance aren't sized for it, things brown out or behave differently at 20% battery than at full.

08 Feet & desk interface The only part of the whole machine that actually touches anything.

What's inside it

  • SkatesPTFE pads. Sometimes glass or ceramic on aftermarket sets.
  • AdhesiveDouble-sided tape, and the reason a badly-applied foot lifts a corner and ruins the glide.
  • Sensor apertureThe window the optics look through.
  • Case screwsVery often underneath the feet — which is why opening a mouse starts with peeling one off.

Why PTFE

It has one of the lowest coefficients of friction of any solid material against almost anything. Virgin PTFE — unfilled and unrecycled — glides best and wears fastest. Filled PTFE lasts considerably longer and feels slower. That's the entire tradeoff, and it's why enthusiasts replace feet as a consumable.

Edge treatment is the underrated detail: a foot with a sharp square edge catches on mousepad weave, while a rounded or chamfered edge rides over it. Two feet of identical material can feel completely different because of it.

09 Auxiliary controls Everything that isn't a main click, and mostly living on its own little board.

What's inside it

  • Side buttonsAlmost always on a separate daughterboard or flex cable, because they sit on a vertical wall the flat mainboard can't reach.
  • DPI buttonFrequently moved to the underside on gaming mice, specifically so it can't be hit mid-game.
  • Mode switchesPower, polling rate, wireless mode. Usually on the bottom.
  • LEDsSurface-mount, on the mainboard.
  • Light pipesMolded clear plastic that ducts light from an LED on the board to wherever you actually see it. A surprising amount of "lighting design" is just plastic geometry.

The daughterboard is the useful pattern to notice here. A flat rigid mainboard can't put a switch on a vertical wall, so you hang a small second board off a flex cable — which also means those parts can be repositioned without touching the main PCB at all. It's the standard escape hatch from the flat-board constraint.

In your build

This is the cheapest freedom you have. Anything on a flex cable can move when the shell shape changes, so put every control your hand touches on a daughterboard and keep the mainboard purely functional.
