01 · Mechanism

The click, and why it can beat the reference

The reference is Logitech's G Pro X2 Superstrike (61 g, 8 K, $179). Under each of its main buttons: an inductive sensor reading sub-millimetre plate travel as a continuous value, and an actuator that fires the click sensation as a waveform. No microswitch anywhere. Actuation point becomes a setting, rapid-trigger clicking falls out for free, and the entire feel of the mouse is firmware.

What we know about how it has to feel

  • The fingertip's vibration band is ~50–500 Hz, peaking near 250 Hz (Pacinian corpuscles). Kilohertz content is inaudible to touch — sharpness is not treble.
  • Sharpness is attack time. An LRA is a resonant mass-spring: it must ring up to amplitude and ring back down, and that rise/fall is the "slight roundness" every Superstrike reviewer reports. A non-resonant actuator with a <2 ms rise attacks like a real leaf-spring snap.
  • The release is half the illusion. A real switch produces a second, lighter event on the way back up. Nearly every haptic implementation skips it; ours fires a distinct release waveform.
  • The press needs a rigid backstop. Force building into something immovable is what sells the illusion (the trackpad trick). Any flex in the plate or shell reads as mush — the CNC'd flexure with a hard stop is load-bearing for feel, not just structure.
  • Sound fuses with feel. Audiotactile integration is documented right in the 200–400 Hz band. The acoustic tick is set by plate/shell resonance and is a design variable, not an accident — target: quiet but defined.

The one EMI problem this build owns

Piezo drive means switching 60–190 V edges centimetres from a 2.4 GHz antenna. Distance, a dedicated ground pour under the drivers, and (if needed) a shield can are the plan — and it's why the layout in §05 puts the HV corner as far from the antenna as the shell allows. This is a real layout problem, not a cost problem; flag it before the first board spin.

If the LRA wins the bench-off

DRV2605L has a fixed I²C address (0x5A) — two of them can't share a bus. One per TWIM instance, no mux in the click path.

03 · Bench forks

Two decisions the P1 rig makes for us

Both are feel judgments, so both get settled by blind pressing on identical plate geometry — plus a measured number (accelerometer on the plate for attack time, GPIO-to-scope for latency) so the winner is defensible.

Fork A — travel sensing

Hall vs inductive

Hall (TMAG5273 or ratiometric analog + magnet on the stem): read straight into the nRF's ADC, sample rate limited only by the ADC, and the driver + calibration code transfers directly to the keyboard project later. Needs temperature compensation; puts magnets near the battery.

Inductive (LDC1101 ×2 + PCB coil + steel tab): the coil is free copper, no magnets, robust to stray fields — the reference implementation. Revised 8/13: the LDC2114 originally slated here was disqualified — its 160 SPS max scan rate (6.25 ms/sample) can't feed a 1.5 ms click budget. The LDC1101 samples up to ~180 kSPS but is single-channel, raw-output silicon: one per button, and baseline tracking moves into our firmware.

  • +Tie-breaker: if they feel the same, Hall wins — it unifies both projects
Fork B — actuator

Piezo vs LRA (vs voice coil)

Piezo: non-resonant, <2 ms to peak, full 1–400 Hz haptic band with independent frequency/amplitude, and 4–10× less power than an LRA (capacitive load + energy-recovery drivers). Directly attacks the "roundness" complaint. Cost penalty is qty-1 economics we mostly dodge; the HV/EMI callout in §02 is the real price.

LRA (wideband, ~150–175 Hz, 8×3 mm): cheap, proven, closed-loop drive with auto-resonance and braking. The fallback that definitely works.

Voice coil (DIY: magnet wire + N35 magnet + CNC flexure): non-resonant like piezo, low-voltage like LRA, buildable in-house for pocket change. Worth one weekend on the rig.

How it runs

One printed jig, one plate geometry, every sensor and actuator candidate mounted interchangeably. Press blind, log attack time and latency, pick. Everything else in this document is solved engineering — this rig is the project.

04 · Scroll

Electromagnetic detents, software coast

No spring arm, no clutch. A toothed steel rim rides between two coils: drive the coils the same direction and flux routes through the teeth — the wheel gets pulled into notch positions (real restoring torque, not friction). Drive them opposed and the fields cancel at the gap — the wheel spins free. Mode toggle is a current flip: instant, silent, nothing moves. Full walkthrough with diagrams lives in the anatomy reference, §03.

  • Auto-shift: firmware already knows wheel velocity from the encoder — flick hard and it drops into freespin, slow down and the ratchet re-engages. Or bind it to a button. Or both.
  • Braking: energizing the coils against rotation makes coast time a software curve, not a bearing property. One-second or four-second spin-downs, per profile.
  • Software detents: count, strength, and asymmetry per application — including detents snapped to content (one per video frame, one per spreadsheet row) and force endstops at the bottom of a page.
  • Flywheel: light printed hub, dense brass ring at the rim (m·r² — put the mass where the radius is). CNC lathe job. 5–8 g of the weight budget, worth every gram.
  • Encoder: magnetic (diametral magnet on the shaft end + on-axis encoder IC). Contactless — a wiper encoder would wear out and miss counts at spinner RPM. Output capped in firmware so freespin doesn't firehose the host.
  • Middle click uses the same Hall-plus-haptic scheme as the mains — the zero-microswitch rule holds everywhere.

05 · Internals

Layout — what goes where, and why

Rough placement, not final routing — but every position below is load-bearing for feel, RF, or balance, so the shape iterations in P4 pack around this arrangement rather than reflowing it.

Placement rationale

  • Sensor · grip centroidThe sensor sets the cursor's rotation center — the biggest single feel decision. Frozen first; everything else packs around it.
  • MCU · front-centerThe antenna feed is the fussiest trace on the board, so the radio side faces the nose and stays short. The sensor SPI can afford to run aft.
  • Antenna · the noseFarthest point from the palm (mostly water) and the battery (metal). The keep-out is a component: no copper, no metal, and plate hardware stays aft of it.
  • HV drivers · mid-leftDiagonal-opposite the antenna, on their own ground pour. Distance is the first EMI defense; a shield can is the fallback.
  • Battery · midline, aft of sensorMass at the grip keeps balance under the fingers and rotational inertia low; midline avoids a yaw bias. Also clear of the RF zone and shell flex.
  • Actuators · on the platesBonded to each plate's underside, soft-mounted to the cradle — the click must come from the plate, not the chassis. Isolation is what makes the illusion local.
  • Scroll · dead centerThe heaviest rotating part sits on the centerline so flicks don't feel handed. Coils flank the rim; the encoder reads the shaft end.
  • Side buttons · FFC daughterboardWall geometry changes with every skin. A flex link means re-shaping a shell never touches the mainboard.
  • Rear · emptyThe hump is pure shell. No mass under the palm heel — balance stays where the hand actually works.
  • USB-C · nose / SWD · rearCable strain lands on the board's strongest edge; debug pads stay reachable with the shell on.

08 · Shells

One chassis, six skins

All the locked geometry — board mounts, sensor aperture, scroll cradle, plate flexures — lives in a rigid internal chassis, identical across all six units. The outer skin is a separate printed part per owner. Re-shaping a skin never touches electronics, and six people get six fits from one mainboard design.

  • Capture the grip, not the hand: each owner squeezes a clay or foam blank in their natural grip; iPhone LiDAR scans it; the scan drives that skin's parameters. An open hand is the wrong shape.
  • Skins are code: CadQuery, parameterized on the component envelope plus hand measurements — a shape change is a re-run, not a remodel.
  • Iterate in PLA, finish in ASA / PA-CF: grip-feel test shells cost an hour each; five or six rounds per person is normal.
  • Texture in the print: fuzzy-skin on grip zones for test shells; modeled texture (rounded bumps ≥0.5 mm, sweat channels) for finals. Reliefs cut shallow-first — a scallop that suggests a finger position forgives; a groove that mandates one punishes. Cut reliefs into the solid before hollowing so wall thickness survives.
  • Undercut grips are legal: no mold to eject from — a pinky shelf the finger hooks under is printable and impossible commercially.

09 · Sequence

Phases

  1. P0

    Tracking, wired

    nRF54L15-DK + PAW3950 module as a USB HID mouse over a cable. Proves the sensor driver and report path.

  2. P1

    Bench rig — both forks

    Runs in parallel with P0. One jig, one plate geometry; Hall and inductive sensing side by side, piezo vs LRA vs voice coil interchangeable. Blind press-off plus measured attack and latency. This rig decides whether the mouse is good.

  3. P2

    The link

    ESB mouse ↔ dongle at 1 kHz, plus the latency rig (GPIO on click-detect into a scope) so every later change is measured, not argued.

  4. P3

    Mainboard

    Custom PCB per §05, panelized with the scroll test board and breakouts. The engineer's phase; the HV corner gets the spectrum-analyzer check here.

  5. P4

    Chassis + six skins

    Hand captures for all six owners, parametric skins, print iterations until each grip is right.

  6. P5

    Config software

    WebHID in the browser — DPI, lift-off, actuation curves, per-button press/release waveforms, scroll detent profiles, coast curves. No installer, no account.

  7. P6

    Frontier — 8K (committed)

    Staged: 4 kHz first on the new High-Speed-USB receiver (existing ACK protocol still fits a 250 µs slot), then 8 kHz via the streaming protocol (no per-packet ACKs, redundant deltas). Mouse hardware already qualifies — nRF54L15 + PAW3950 were chosen for this. Plus coil-brake tuning, content-aware detents, and whatever the rig taught us. Battery reality: 8K is a per-profile mode, not the default.

View

The current mouse, in layers

One machine, four levels of dress. Levels are cumulative — each adds onto the one before, the way the mouse actually assembles. Layers 1–3 are identical across all six units; only layer 4 differs per owner.

Plan view, front at top. The dashed silhouette is the eventual skin, ghosted for context.

Level 1 — the board

Everything electrical. This layer is identical across all six units, and every position on it is load-bearing for RF, feel, or balance.

  • nRF54L15 with its radio side facing the nose; the hatched teal zone is the antenna keep-out — no copper, no metal, ever.
  • PAW3950 at the grip centroid — the cursor's rotation center, frozen before anything else is placed.
  • Battery on the midline aft of the sensor, fuel gauge at its edge; balance stays under the fingers.
  • Amber corner: BOS1901 ×2 + boost — the high-voltage piezo drivers, diagonal-opposite the antenna.
  • USB-C and charge IC at the nose; SWD debug pads at the board's rear.

Level 2 — the mechanisms

The moving parts. Still identical across all six.

  • Button plates (dashed — they float above the board) with piezo patches bonded to their undersides and Hall magnets on their stems.
  • The electromagnetic scroll: detent coils flanking the toothed steel rim, magnetic encoder on the shaft end, middle click beneath, brass-rim flywheel under the slot.
  • Side-button daughterboard on the left wall, linked to the mainboard by FFC — it moves freely when skins change shape.

Level 3 — the chassis

The rigid frame that holds layers 1 and 2 in a fixed relationship. Identical ×6, printed dense and stiff.

  • Frame and four screw bosses — every critical vertical dimension in the mouse is measured from this part.
  • Scroll cradle, flexure anchor bars with hard stops, and the sensor aperture ring.
  • Stiffness here is feel: any flex under the plates reads as a mushy click, so this layer takes the ribs and the walls.

Level 4 — the skin

The only layer that differs per person. Six shapes, one chassis underneath.

  • Printed grip texture on both flanks — rounded bumps with sweat channels, fuzzy-skin on test prints.
  • Wheel opening and button split lines are cut into the skin, not the chassis.
  • Right-flank pinky shelf, undercut so the finger hooks under it — printable, impossible to injection-mold.
  • Weight is a skin parameter too: denser infill where an owner wants heft, same chassis regardless.

Reading the two reference columns

The Superstrike column is us with a factory: same architecture (inductive sensing + haptic click + proprietary 2.4 GHz), commodity-priced. The Apple column is the opposite philosophy on every row — Bluetooth over dongle, month-long battery over milliseconds, touch surface over wheel, one real switch over zero. Where we differ from Logitech it's by choice (piezo over LRA, EM detents, hand-fit skins); where we differ from Apple it's the entire premise.

Deep dive

Radio, antennas, and dongles

The goal, stated precisely: no delay or loss a human can feel. That decomposes into three measurable things — low latency (the trip takes little time), low jitter (every trip takes the same time — humans notice inconsistency far more than a constant offset), and loss recovery faster than perception (a retried packet that arrives 400 µs late never existed, as far as your hand knows).

Why proprietary 2.4 GHz (ESB) and not Bluetooth

Bluetooth is a negotiated protocol: the host controls connection intervals, and consistent sub-millisecond timing is somewhere between awkward and impossible — it's why the Magic Mouse is a month-per-charge device and not a gaming device. Nordic's Enhanced ShockBurst is barely a protocol at all: our firmware owns every microsecond — when packets fly, how retries work, which channels to use. Every serious gaming mouse (Lightspeed included) made the same call. The cost is the dongle in the box; the payoff is that timing becomes engineering instead of negotiation.

The full latency chain — where the microseconds go

HopWhat happensBudget (est.)
SenseHall/ADC read of click travel, or motion delta from PAW3950 over SPI10–50 µs
PackFirmware assembles the HID report (buttons bitfield + dX/dY + wheel)<10 µs
AirESB packet, 2 Mbps PHY: transmit + ACK round trip~200–300 µs
DongleReceive, translate to USB HID, queue for next USB service<50 µs
USBHost polls the interrupt endpoint — Full-Speed: every 1 ms; High-Speed: every 125 µs0–1000 µs (FS)

Read the last row twice: at 1 kHz the USB service interval dominates the whole chain — everything else together is ~0.3 ms while USB alone can add up to 1 ms of waiting. That's why the 4K/8K upgrade is a dongle project (High-Speed USB shrinks that row 8×), and why the mouse side barely changes. It's also why synchronizing matters: firing our radio packet just before the USB poll (rather than randomly within the window) converts up to a millisecond of jitter into a constant — "motion sync," and we get to implement it properly because we own both ends.

Packets, retries, and dodging Wi-Fi

  • Every packet is ACKed. No ACK within ~a hundred µs → retransmit immediately. At 1 kHz there's room for 2–3 retries inside a single polling window — a lost packet is usually repaired before the host would ever have asked for it.
  • Channel agility: 2.4 GHz is shared with every Wi-Fi network and microwave nearby. The link keeps a ranked channel map (Wi-Fi ch. 1/6/11 leave known quiet gaps), monitors failure rate per channel, and hops when a channel sours — the dongle coordinates so both ends move together.
  • Payloads stay tiny (≤10 bytes) — shorter airtime means lower collision odds and cheaper retries. The keyboard adds a second report type (key bitmap + analog values on change) on the same scheme.
  • TX-then-tap: the click packet transmits ~200 µs before the haptic fires, so the most important packet flies in electrical silence. Free, in firmware.

Tuning is not optional: an antenna is only as good as its matching network, and the matching shifts when the shell, hand, and battery arrive around it. The plan: measure with a VNA (university lab) with the shell on and a hand on the mouse, adjust the two or three matching components, re-measure. One afternoon, and it's the difference between the −40 dBm link margin story being true or theoretical. The keyboard is easier — bigger board, more distance from hands to antenna placement options.

The dongles

  • v1 — nRF52840 Dongle ($10): ESB on one side, USB HID at 1 kHz on the other. Both mouse and keyboard v1 live here.
  • One dongle, both devices: ESB supports multiple pipes — mouse on pipe 0, keyboard on pipe 1, one USB device with two HID interfaces. Two packets per millisecond is well within air budget. Fewer dongles, and inter-device tricks (mouse-click-plus-key macros) become possible at the receiver.
  • Frontier — High-Speed USB receiver (P6): 125 µs microframes unlock 4–8 kHz. Candidates: nRF54H20 (radio + HS USB in one — verify the datasheet path first) or an STM32F723-class MCU bridging to a Nordic radio. This is where "8K" actually lives.
  • Placement matters more than specs: a dongle behind a metal PC case can lose 20+ dB versus one on a short USB extender at desk level with line-of-sight to the mouse. Ship each unit with a $2 extender and the radio problem halves.

The 8K commitment — staged path

Decided 8/13: 8K polling is a committed goal, not a maybe. The chain audit says most of it is already paid for: the nRF54L15 has the 125 µs loop headroom, the PAW3950 is native-8K, and both click-sensing forks sample far faster than 8 kHz. What remains is two projects:

  • Protocol redesign (firmware): the 1 kHz send-ACK-retry scheme can't fit a 125 µs slot (the ACK round trip alone is ~200–300 µs). 8K switches to streaming — transmit every slot on the faster nRF54 proprietary PHY, no per-packet ACKs, each packet carrying the last few deltas so loss self-heals. A few weekends of protocol work, tuned on the P2 latency rig.
  • The High-Speed dongle (hardware): the real build — nRF54H20 (radio + HS USB in one, verify the SDK path) or STM32F723-class + Nordic radio bridge. Custom board, 90 Ω differential-pair routing, microframe HID firmware. A couple months of evenings, ~$50–100 in prototypes.
  • The free intermediate: once the HS dongle exists, 4 kHz needs no protocol change — a 250 µs slot still fits the proven ACK scheme. Ship 1 kHz → 4 kHz → 8 kHz, each step validated on the rig before anyone depends on it.
  • Battery honesty: 8K radio duty collapses battery life (the Superstrike's 90 h at 1 kHz is a fraction at 8K; ours will behave the same). 8K ships as a per-profile toggle, 1 kHz stays the daily default.
  • Perspective: the prize is ~0.9 ms of staleness removed — real, measurable, and dessert. Click latency (P1–P2) remains where feel actually lives, which is why 8K is sequenced last rather than first.
